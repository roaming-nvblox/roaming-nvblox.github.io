Abstract

Robots need maps that remain consistent through motion and occlusion. We present ROAMing, a causal RGB-D mapper that maintains an adaptive surfel map without object segmentation. A local-relative transformer refines lifted optical flow and propagates motion to non-visible neighbors; depth fusion adds newly observed surfaces and removes stale geometry. We introduce ArenaPickPlace, a synthetic robot-interaction dataset with complete surface trajectories. Our fast configuration achieves 88.3% completion of currently hidden surfaces on objects that move during the sequence. Evaluation includes zero-shot Sim4D reconstruction and real-world PW-DROID tracking. ROAMing trades some tracking accuracy relative to non-causal trackers for immediate updates. With precomputed optical flow, fast tracking and fusion take 56 ms per ArenaPickPlace frame.

Method

At each time step ROAMing receives a posed RGB-D frame and updates its surfel map in two stages. It first warps every existing surfel, visible or hidden, with a predicted 3D motion, and only then fuses the new depth image. Because the map is moved before association, moving objects do not corrupt data association, and surfaces that pass behind an occluder keep travelling with their neighbours instead of being frozen or discarded.

Motion refinement before fusion. Source attention uses a fixed neighborhood graph; warped attention recomputes neighbors as the motion estimate changes. Three refinement iterations predict a residual for every surfel, including those with no visible correspondence.

Geometric prior. Forward optical flow between frames t−1 and t (RAFT in the fast configuration, WAFT in the accurate one) is lifted to 3D with depth and the camera poses. This supplies motion wherever a valid correspondence exists; hidden surfels and rejected correspondences receive a zero prior, so the prior alone can never move hidden geometry.

Visibility-aware refinement. A local-relative transformer, in the spirit of Point Transformer and RAFT’s iterative refinement, predicts a residual displacement for every surfel. Tokens encode the prior, its magnitude, visibility and k-nearest-neighbour distance; attention encodes relative offsets rather than absolute positions. Each of three iterations attends over a fixed source graph (k = 16), warps the centres by the current estimate, rebuilds a warped graph (k = 32) and attends again. A hidden surfel remains a token even with a zero prior, so motion propagates through partially observed local structure without any segmentation. Motion below 2 mm is zeroed to suppress drift on static geometry.

Surfel fusion. Established projective surfel fusion is applied to the propagated map: an information filter combines depth-consistent associations, uncovered measurements spawn new surfels, free-space contradictions prune stale ones, and occluded or out-of-frustum surfels persist.

ArenaPickPlace dataset

Existing 4D reconstruction datasets do not label ground-truth surface points per time step as static or dynamic and visible or hidden, and 3D tracking benchmarks only provide trajectories for pixel-aligned points visible in the first frame. ArenaPickPlace fills that gap: 38 tabletop manipulation scenes, each with 10 episode initializations and 10 cameras, captured as 200-frame RGB-D sequences rendered with ray tracing in Isaac Lab Arena. Every sequence ships with depth, optical flow, meshes, 6-DoF poses, and complete surface point and normal trajectories labelled static/dynamic and visible/hidden, including points that become occluded or leave the camera view. We train on all episodes and views of 17 scenes and test on the first episode and camera of the remaining scenes.

Dynamic reconstruction

We compare with 4DTAM and DynaGSLAM. Accuracy (map → ground truth) and completion (ground truth → map) count the fraction of points within 3 cm of the other set, scoring only ever-observed geometry. Dynamic labels objects that move at any scored frame; hidden includes occluded, back-facing and out-of-view points. The methods differ most on what they do with geometry that stops being observed, so the dynamic-hidden column is where ROAMing’s warp-before-fuse design shows: with 31k versus 30k primitives, fast ROAMing improves dynamic-hidden completion over DynaGSLAM from 60.7% to 88.3%, and tracks six times more dynamic-hidden primitives (6.1% vs 1.0% of the map).

Zero-shot transfer to Sim4D

Sim4D contains animated non-rigid meshes rendered over randomized textures, well outside the training distribution in geometry, motion and appearance. Applied zero-shot and measured by held-out-view depth L1 where both rendered and ground-truth depth are valid, accurate ROAMing and 4DTAM have similar mean error (1.77 vs 1.79 cm) across eight sequences; the fast configuration reaches 2.08 cm, and DynaGSLAM 7.59 cm.

Causal 3D tracking

The tracking evaluation follows first-frame query identities through the sequence, without fusion, spawning or pruning. Track4World processes the whole video and uses ground-truth scale alignment; TAPIP3D uses 16-frame windows with stride 8, so it sees the future. ROAMing updates from frames t−1 and t only and trades a few millimetres of error for immediate, causal estimates. On the real-world PW-DROID clips we restore the robot-arm annotations that the released pseudo ground truth omits.

What each component contributes

Component behavior. Geometric flow leaves hidden points behind (a); refinement propagates them (b); fusion adds tracks on the disoccluded bottle (c).

The geometric prior can only move surfels that are visible in both frames, so points that pass behind the arm are left frozen in place. The refiner gives every surfel a token and lets hidden ones borrow motion from their visible neighbours, which carries them along the arm’s trajectory. Fusion then corrects accumulated drift against new depth, removes surfels that free-space observations contradict, and begins tracking surfaces the moment they are revealed. Trackers that follow only first-frame queries never pick up the bottle at all.

Limitations

Reconstruction is evaluated only in simulation; training and the main benchmark use fixed cameras, leaving camera motion untested. The system depends on external poses and optical flow, and cannot infer arbitrary motion of fully hidden components disconnected from visible local structure. Real-world PW-DROID results evaluate 12-frame tracking clips, not the long-term accuracy and completeness of persistent maps.
